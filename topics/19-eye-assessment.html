👁️ Eye Assessment Framework

Acute eye conditions are NOT in NSW PATH expanded scope (May 2026) · Pharmacist role: recognise red flags, supply S3 chloramphenicol for uncomplicated bacterial conjunctivitis, escalate everything else · See also 13-conjunctivitis
💡 The Decision Support Tool 200 (DST-200) framework referenced below is a Canadian (British Columbia) Remote Nursing tool — used here as a structural template only, NOT NSW protocol. Use it to organise your approach but verify against current Australian guidance.

Specific emergencies

⛔ Acute angle-closure glaucoma: eye pain + headache + N/V + halos + cloudy cornea + mid-dilated unreactive pupil → EMERGENCY. Sight-threatening within hours.

Chemical splash: IRRIGATE COPIOUSLY ≥20 min (≥30 min for alkali — penetrates deep; target pH 7.0–7.4 on litmus) THEN refer.

Contact lens wearer + red eye = keratitis (esp. Pseudomonas) until proven otherwise.

Neonate <28 days with conjunctivitis (Chlamydia, Gonorrhoea — sight-threatening).

Hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis = Gonorrhoea (sight-threatening).

Trauma incl. UV (welder, snow) — assess for globe rupture; do NOT palpate globe.

✅ Pharmacist-managed presentations (limited)

  • Uncomplicated bacterial conjunctivitis (≥2 yo per current S3 chloramphenicol protocol) — see 13-conjunctivitis
  • Allergic conjunctivitis — olopatadine 0.1% drops BD, ketotifen, cromoglycate, cool compress
  • Dry eye / irritation — artificial tears, ocular lubricant
  • Stye / blepharitis (mild) — warm compress, lid hygiene
  • Foreign body sensation without injury (cleared by blink, no fluorescein uptake suspected)

📋 Structured eye assessment (DST-200 Red Eye framework — Canadian template, NOT NSW protocol)

Step 1 — Visual acuity FIRST

  • Distance with handheld card or count fingers
  • Compare each eye separately
  • Establishes baseline + screens for sight-threatening pathology
  • Any unilateral acuity drop = refer

Step 2 — External inspection

StructureLook for
LidSwelling, erythema, vesicles (HSV/VZV), nodules, ptosis
ConjunctivaHyperaemia distribution (diffuse vs ciliary flush vs sectoral)
CorneaClarity, light reflection, foreign body
ScleraColour (icterus), redness (scleritis = deep, painful)
PupilSize, shape (irregular = uveitis/synechiae), reactivity (PERRLA), anisocoria

Step 3 — Discharge characterisation

  • Watery vs mucoid vs purulent vs blood
  • Bilateral vs unilateral
  • Eyelids stuck on waking? (bacterial)
  • Itch dominant? (allergic)

Step 4 — Symptom triage

Pain / vision profileLikely
Mild/no pain + mild blurring / normal visionHyperaemia / conjunctivitis (sub-categorise by discharge type)
Moderate-severe pain + vision loss / distorted pupil / corneal involvementHSV keratitis, hyperacute bacterial, keratitis, corneal ulcer, acute angle-closure glaucoma, iritis, traumatic injury, chemical burn, scleritis → emergency referral

Step 5 — Focused history

  • Recent contacts with similar symptoms; recent URTI
  • Contact lens use (wear schedule, hygiene, sleeping in lenses)
  • Trauma, chemical, UV exposure
  • Sexual history (STI risk for hyperacute/subacute)
  • PMH: atopy, autoimmune, immunocompromise, herpes
  • Medications (e.g. anticoagulant + subconjunctival haemorrhage)

Step 6 — Examination caveats

⛔ DO NOT palpate globe if rupture injury suspected or recent eye surgery.
Fluorescein with cobalt blue light for suspected abrasion (refer for this — not pharmacist scope).
Pre-auricular lymph node palpation.

📋 Common presentations — distinguishing features

Bacterial conjunctivitis

  • Conjunctiva erythematous (uni- or bilateral)
  • Chemosis if severe
  • Purulent discharge with crusting
  • PERRLA, visual acuity normal
  • Pre-auricular nodes palpable in N. gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia, MRSA
  • Pathogens: H. influenzae, S. pneumoniae, N. gonorrhoeae, Pseudomonas, S. aureus, Chlamydia

Viral conjunctivitis

  • Preceded by viral URTI
  • Watery / mucoid discharge
  • PERRLA, visual acuity normal
  • Swollen eyelids
  • Enlarged tender preauricular nodes
  • 1–4 days; infectious 10–12 days from onset while eyes red
  • Often unilateral → bilateral within 24–48 h
  • Causes >80% acute conjunctivitis (adenovirus, coxsackie, enterovirus 70, HSV, EBV, less often VZV, measles, rubella)
  • Dendritic keratitis on fluorescein = HSV → refer

Corneal abrasion — recognition

  • Trauma history
  • Sudden unilateral eye pain (sharp / worse with blinking)
  • Mild blurred vision (from tearing)
  • Mild photophobia
  • Moderate-profuse tearing
  • Foreign body sensation
  • Contact lens use risk
  • Fluorescein stain — damaged corneal cells stain green; cobalt blue light visualises abrasion
  • Rust ring may form around metallic/iron foreign body
  • → Refer for assessment + topical anaesthetic (NOT for ongoing patient use)

🧪 Chemical injury (do FIRST, then refer)

  • IRRIGATE COPIOUSLY with saline or water ≥20 min
  • ≥30 min for alkali burns (penetrate deep; target pH 7.0–7.4 on litmus)
  • Continuous irrigation while transferring to ED
  • Lubricant drops post
  • Refer all chemical injuries — alkali especially severe

🩸 Subconjunctival haemorrhage

  • Painless, sectoral red patch, no vision change
  • Self-limiting 1–2 weeks
  • Causes: Valsalva, cough, trauma, HTN, anticoagulant
  • Refer if recurrent, painful, vision affected, post-trauma

💧 Eye drop technique (counsel)

  1. Hand hygiene
  2. Tilt head back, look up
  3. Pull lower lid down → pocket
  4. 1 drop in pocket (don't touch dropper to eye)
  5. Close eye; gentle pressure inner corner 1 min
  6. Wait ≥5 min between different drops

🎯 OSCE pearls

  • Visual acuity FIRST (both eyes separately)
  • Pupil shape + reactivity (PERRLA)
  • Pain + photophobia + vision change = sight-threatening
  • Contact lens question always
  • Hyperaemia pattern: diffuse vs ciliary flush
  • Don't touch globe if rupture suspected
  • DST-200 framework is Canadian — structural template only, not NSW protocol

⚠️ Pitfalls

  • Contact lens + red eye = keratitis until proven otherwise
  • Headache + N/V + halos = angle-closure glaucoma (emergency, not "just a headache")
  • Steroid drops → catastrophic in HSV keratitis (worsens dendrite)
  • Topical anaesthetic given for ongoing use → corneal toxicity, neurotrophic ulcer
